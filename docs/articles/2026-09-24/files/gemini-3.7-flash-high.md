### The "Hidden" Difference: Width + Flat Grind

Both knives have a **Flat Grind** (the metal tapers in a straight line from the 5 mm thick top spine down to the sharp edge). 

* **Knife Wide (40 mm wide):** Because the blade is wider, it has more room to taper down from 5 mm to zero. This creates a **gentler, thinner cutting angle**, allowing it to slice through materials with less resistance.
* **Knife Long (34 mm wide):** Because the blade is narrower, it tapers more abruptly from 5 mm. This creates a **steeper, wedge-like angle**, which is great for splitting wood apart but will push through dense foods (like apples or potatoes) like a wedge rather than a clean slice.

### Survival Tasks: What, When, How, and Knife Performance

---

#### 1. Batoning (Splitting Firewood)
* **What it is:** Splitting logs of wood by hammering your knife through them using a thick branch (a "baton") instead of an axe.
* **When you do it:** After rain or snow, the outside of fallen wood is wet. You baton logs open to reach the **bone-dry wood inside** to start a fire.
* **How you do it:** Stand a log upright, place your blade across the top, and firmly strike the back (spine) of the knife blade with a heavy wooden stick until it splits the wood down the middle.
* **The Winner: Knife Long**
  * *Why:* To baton safely, your blade must be longer than the log is thick so the knife tip sticks out the other side for you to hit. Knife Long’s **127 mm blade** can split logs up to 3–4 inches thick. Knife Wide (105 mm) is too short for medium logs and should only be used on thin branches.

---

#### 2. Carving & Making "Feathersticks"
* **What it is:** Shaving very thin, paper-like curls into a stick of wood without detaching them, creating a "wooden flower" called a featherstick. It also includes carving tent stakes, tool handles, and trap triggers.
* **When you do it:** Feathersticks are the #1 way to catch a spark from a ferro rod to start a campfire.
* **How you do it:** Hold the stick firmly, angle your knife at about 15–20 degrees, and push down gently with controlled, repeating strokes to peel micro-thin curls.
* **The Winner: Knife Wide**
  * *Why:* When carving, leverage is everything. The shorter **105 mm blade** keeps your cutting edge closer to your hand, giving you maximum control and less wrist strain. Additionally, the gentler flat-grind angle cuts into wood curls smoothly.

---

#### 3. Chopping & Delimbing
* **What it is:** Hacking through branches to clear a trail, harvest shelter poles, or trim side branches off firewood.
* **When you do it:** When building an emergency lean-to shelter or clearing a campsite.
* **How you do it:** Hold the knife loosely toward the back of the handle to maximize your swing, strike the branch at a 45-degree angle (never flat 90 degrees), and use fluid wrist-snaps.
* **The Winner: Knife Long**
  * *Why:* Chopping requires **weight and length** to build momentum. Knife Long is heavier (**285 g**) and longer (**254 mm total**), making it far more capable of light chopping than Knife Wide.

---

#### 4. Slicing & Camp Food Prep
* **What it is:** Slicing meat, cutting vegetables, filleting fish, or cutting cordage/rope.
* **When you do it:** Every day around camp during meal prep and gear setup.
* **How you do it:** Drawing the sharp edge across the item in smooth, forward-and-back motions.
* **The Winner: Knife Wide (for general food/skinning) / Knife Long (for bread/large meat)**
  * *Why:* Knife Wide’s thinner edge geometry (due to its 40 mm width) slices through food with less wedging. It also has a wider "belly" (the curved portion of the blade), which is excellent for skinning game. Knife Long wins if you need to slice through large loaves of bread or big cuts of meat in a single pass.

---

#### 5. Tip Work & Drilling (Making Holes)
* **What it is:** Using the pointed tip of the knife to bore holes into wood or leather.
* **When you do it:** Making the baseboard for a primitive bow-drill friction fire, making gear repairs, or crafting wooden traps.
* **How you do it:** Press the tip into the material and twist the knife back and forth like a drill bit.
* **The Winner: Tie**
  * *Why:* Both knives have a **Drop Point** shape. A drop point lowers the tip toward the center line of the blade, creating a strong, thick point that will not snap easily when twisted in hard wood.

### Handles and Steels: Real-World Differences

* **Micarta vs. Olive Wood:**
  * **Knife Long (Micarta):** Micarta is canvas soaked in resin. It is completely immune to water, snow, and mud. In fact, it gets *grippier* when wet, making it safer for intense, sweaty survival chores.
  * **Knife Wide (Olive Wood):** Beautiful, warm, and natural. However, wood can become slick when coated in fish slime or water, and it can shrink or crack over time if left wet.

* **Niolox vs. Böhler N-695:**
  * **Knife Wide (Niolox):** A modern high-performance steel. It holds an edge longer and resists chipping, but takes more effort to sharpen once dull.
  * **Knife Long (Böhler N-695):** A classic, tough stainless steel. While it may dull slightly faster than Niolox, it is much easier to bring back to razor-sharpness using a simple pocket sharpening stone at camp.

### Summary: Which One Should You Pick?

* **Choose Knife Wide if:**
  * Your main focus is **bushcraft, carving, making feathersticks, skinning game, and camp cooking**.
  * You prefer a compact, lightweight knife that rides comfortably on your belt all day.

* **Choose Knife Long if:**
  * Your main focus is **all-around wilderness survival, splitting dry firewood (batoning), and shelter building**.
  * You want an all-weather tool with an indestructible grip (Micarta) that is easy to sharpen in the field.
